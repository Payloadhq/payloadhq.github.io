In a waterfall, sequence is arithmetic.

Distribution fee, expenses, recoupment, participation: each step takes its cut of what the previous step left. The same percentages in a different order pay different people, which is why waterfall disputes are usually about order, not rates.

The structure

Every waterfall step needs three definitions

A waterfall step that says only "30 percent" is under-specified. To compute it the same way twice, a step needs its rate, its base (the gross, or the remainder after a named earlier step), and its position in the sequence. Film distribution deals, content platform settlements, franchise royalty structures, and joint venture agreements all share this shape, and most statement disputes trace back to one of the three being ambiguous in the contract or improvised in the spreadsheet.

Recoupment sits inside the waterfall as a stateful step: an advance or minimum guarantee is recovered from a named participant's share, event by event, until it clears, and that participant's position in the sequence determines how fast it clears. Recoupment mechanics in detail.

How RevRule handles it

The waterfall is the rule set

A RevRule Revenue Graph is an ordered set of entitlement rules: each step names its base and rate, recoupment steps carry their balances as engine state, and effective-dated versions handle amendments without rewriting settled history. Questions

Waterfalls, answered plainly

What is a revenue waterfall in a contract?

A waterfall is an ordered sequence of deductions and splits applied to revenue. A typical film or content waterfall takes the distributor or platform share first, then fees, then recoupable expenses and advances, and only then divides what remains among the participation holders. Each step is computed on the remainder of the step before it.

Why does the order of a waterfall matter so much?

Because most steps are percentages of a remainder, not of the gross. Moving a fee before or after an expense changes the base every later step is computed on. Two statements can cite the same percentages and pay different amounts purely because the sequence differs, which is why participation disputes so often come down to order rather than rates.

How should a waterfall be documented so participants can verify it?

As an explicit ordered rule set, with each step named, its base defined (gross, or the remainder after a named step), and its rate fixed, plus a per-event ledger that shows the input, each step's amount, and the remainder passed down. If a participant cannot recompute one event from the published rules, the documentation is not finished.
